Exploring 250 Years of the American Story

Alliances That Sustain America is a student essay contest examining how the relationship between Europe and the United States has shaped the American story. Open to Fairfax County Public Schools students in grades 7–12, the contest invites students to investigate a historical period, event, alliance, or area of cooperation and use the evidence they discover to develop their own conclusions.

The Langley World Affairs Club developed the initiative in partnership with the Delegation of the European Union to the United States in recognition of America's 250th anniversary. It builds on the club's 2025 Bernardo de Gálvez Essay Contest, developed with the Embassy of Spain in Washington, D.C., which explored Spain's role in American independence.

Before you begin

Dear Students,

Whether you have entered essay contests before or this is your first one, I hope you will take part.

You do not need to be an expert on Europe, diplomacy, or American history to begin. Start with a topic that interests you. Your topic can come from any period in the history of the relationship between Europe and the United States and can explore areas such as science and innovation, culture, diplomacy, education, and beyond.

This is an analytical historical essay, not a persuasive one. Your job is to investigate a part of this history, examine the evidence, explain why it mattered, and let your research lead you to your own conclusion.

First, second, and third place will be awarded in both the middle school and high school divisions, and award recipients will be invited to the Delegation of the European Union to the United States in Washington, D.C., for a recognition event.

If this is your first essay contest, that is completely fine. You do not have to figure everything out at once. The six steps below will walk you through the process, from choosing your focus to submitting your essay.

Ask for help along the way. Good researchers and writers do. Teachers, parents or guardians, librarians, tutors, older students, and AI tools can help you explore topics, find reliable sources, think through evidence, and get feedback. The important part is that the thinking, conclusions, and final writing remain yours.

If you are not sure how to ask someone for help, here is a message you can send them.

“I'm entering a historical essay contest about how the relationship between Europe and the United States has shaped the American story. It is an analytical historical essay, not a persuasive one. I'm supposed to investigate the history, examine the evidence, explain why it mattered, and let my research lead me to my own conclusion. Would you look at the contest page with me? I'd really appreciate your help choosing a topic, finding reliable sources, thinking through the evidence, and giving me feedback as I write and revise.”

I hope you'll take part. Follow your curiosity, trust the research process, and see where the evidence leads you.

How to enter, in six steps

1

Understand the prompt

How has the relationship between Europe and the United States shaped the American story?

Choose a historical period, event, alliance, or area of cooperation between Europe and the United States. Investigate what brought the two sides together, what challenged or changed their relationship, and why it mattered to the United States.

Use historical evidence to develop your analysis and reach your own conclusion.

2

Know the requirements

Entries. One essay per student.

Length. Up to 650 words. Your title, citations, and bibliography don't count toward the limit.

Sources. Use reliable sources, cite them, and include a brief bibliography.

Help and AI. Getting help is encouraged, whether from people or AI tools. Keep track of the assistance you receive, because the submission form will ask you to describe it. See Help, AI, and original work.

3

Plan and write

Plan first. Choose your focus, do your research, and organize your ideas before you write.

Analyze, don't persuade. Don't start with a position you're trying to prove. Examine what shaped the relationship, what challenged or changed it, and why it mattered, and let the evidence lead you to your conclusion.

Use evidence. Support your analysis with specific evidence, such as a document, date, decision, fact, or statistic, rather than general statements about the period.

Title it last. Once the essay is finished, give it a title that reflects its focus.

4

Submit your essay

Check your essay against the Step 2 requirements, then complete the online submission form by Tuesday, December 1, 2026, at 11:59 p.m. ET. You will paste your title, essay, and sources directly into the form; no document upload is required.

Open the submission form
5

What makes a strong essay

A strong essay does more than tell what happened or argue for a position. It starts with the history, follows the evidence, explains why the evidence matters, and reaches a conclusion based on what the research shows. There is no predetermined conclusion; what matters is how carefully you examine the evidence and how well it supports the conclusion you reach.

Historical understanding
Shows an accurate understanding of what was happening at the time.
Analysis
Explains why events mattered, not just what happened.
Use of evidence
Uses specific, reliable evidence to explain and support its ideas.
Organization
Presents ideas clearly and in a logical order.
Clarity of writing
Communicates ideas clearly, directly, and effectively.

Help, AI, and original work

Getting help is encouraged. Teachers, parents or guardians, librarians, tutors, other students, and AI tools can help you research, explore ideas, understand evidence, and get feedback.

AI is welcome as a tool. If you use ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, or another generative AI tool, keep track of which tool you used and how it helped. The submission form will ask you to describe the assistance you received and how you used AI, if applicable.

The bottom line. Whether the help comes from a person or from AI, the thinking, conclusions, and final writing must be your own. Assistance is encouraged. Substitution is not.
